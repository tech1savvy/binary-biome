# Overview

- **Patent** – A *statutory grant* by the government for new inventions.
- **Monopoly Rights** – Grants *exclusive rights* to the inventor for a defined period.
- **Disclosure Requirement** – The inventor must *fully disclose* the *invention*.
- Legal Protection – Prevents others from using, making, or selling the invention without permission.
- **Territorial Rights** – Patent rights apply only *within the country* where they are granted.
- **Term** – Valid for *20 years* from the date of filing.

# Aim of Patents

- Encourage industrial development.
- Promote complete disclosure* of inventions.
- Prevent duplication of existing innovations.
- Ensure commercial relevance of new inventions.
- Protect researchers from exploitation.
- Enable revenue generation for inventors.
- Serve as a source of technical information for further advancements.

# What can be Patented

- **Process or Method** – A new way of doing something or a unique process.
- **Machine or Apparatus** – A novel device or system that performs a specific function.
- **Article of Manufacture** – A tangible, man-made product.
- **Composition of Matter** – Includes chemical compounds and physical mixtures:
    - **Chemical Compounds** – New chemical formulations or molecular structures.
    - **Physical Mixtures** – Unique combinations of substances that create a new product.
- **==Improvements==** – Enhancements or modifications to any of the above categories.

# Patentability Criteria

## **Novelty**

- > The invention must be *new* and *not disclosed in any prior act* i.e. should *not* exist *already* *in* the *world* in any form.
- The invention must not have been:
    - Published anywhere in the world before the patent application.
    - Previously known or used publicly, even if not patented or published.
    - Used, sold, manufactured, or developed anywhere in the world before the patent application.
    - Claimed in any prior patent specification globally.
- **Novelty Search** – Conducted to *check if* the *invention* has *already* been *disclosed* in prior art.

## Industrial Utility
- Capable of being made or used in industry.
- Can be reproduced with the same characteristics multiple times.

## **Non-Obviousness**
- Should not be obvious to a person skilled in the relevant field.
- Must demonstrate:
    - Technical advancement
    - Economic significance

## Subject Matter
- The invention must fall within the categories of patentable subject matter as defined by law.

# What are Anticipations
- Meaning with Respect to Patents:
	- > Any *prior description* or disclosure *of* the *invention* that removes its novelty.
	- If an invention is already known or documented, it cannot be patented.

# Application for Patents

## Who Can File a Patent? (IPA Section 6: Persons Entitled to Apply)

1. *True and first inventor* of the invention
2. *Assignee* of the true and first inventor
3. *Legal representative* of a deceased person who was entitled to apply before death

- Application can be submitted alone or jointly with others.

## Types of Applications

- **Ordinary Application** – A standard patent application filed *without* claiming *priority* from any other application.
- **Convention Application** – Filed to *claim priority* *over* an *application* *filed* in a convention country (part of a international group of countries) *within 12 months*.
- **Patent of Addition Application** – Filed *for improvements* or modifications *to* an *existing* *patent*ed invention.
- **Divisional Application** – Filed when an *original application contains multiple inventions* and *needs to* be *divide*d.
- **PCT International Phase Application** – Filed under the *Patent Cooperation Treaty* (PCT) for *international patent* protection.
- **PCT National Phase Application** – *Filed* in specific countries *after* the *international phase* of a *PCT* application *to seek national protection* in those nations.

### Provisional Application

- Provides *early priority* for the invention.  
- *Less expensive* compared to a complete application.
- *Quick process* to establish a filing date.
- Includes only *title and description* of the invention.
- *No claims* are *required* in a provisional application.
- *Cannot be filed* for Convention, PCT, or Divisional applications.
- *Optional but advisable* to secure an early priority date.

#### Complete Specification
- Where an application for a patent includes a provisional specification, a complete specification must be filed within *twelve months* from the filing date.

## Documents Required for Filing a Patent Application

1. **Application for Grant of Patent (Form 1)** – Submitted in duplicate.
2. **Complete/Provisional Specification (Form 2)** – Submitted in duplicate, detailing the invention.
3. **Statement and Undertaking (Form 3)** – Declaration regarding foreign applications.
4. **Power of Attorney (Form 26)** – Required if the application is filed through an attorney.
5. **Declaration of Inventorship (Form 5)** – Confirms the inventor's contribution.
6. **Requisite Statutory Fees** – Payment proof (cheque/DD).
7. **Covering Letter** – Lists all submitted documents.

## Specification

### Contents
- Title of the invention
- Field of the invention
- Background of the invention (Prior Art)
- Object of the invention
- Summary of the invention
- Brief description of drawings, if any
- Detailed description of the invention
###
- > Claims (not required in a provisional application)
- > Abstract (not required in a provisional application)

# Patent Filing to Grant Process

## Filing of Application
- The patent application can be filed as a provisional or complete specification.
- If a provisional specification is filed, the complete specification must be filed within 12 months.

## Publication of Application
- The patent application is published after 18 months from the priority date unless early publication is requested.

## Request for Examination (RFE)
- The applicant must file an examination request within 48 months from the priority date or filing date, whichever is earlier.
- For a divisional application, the RFE must be filed within 6 months from the filing of the further application.

## Examination & FER (First Examination Report)
- The Controller refers the application to an Examiner within 1 month of publication or receipt of RFE.
- The Examiner prepares the examination report within 1-3 months.
- The Controller disposes of the Examiner's report within 1 month of receipt.
- The First Examination Report (FER) is issued within 6 months of the RFE or publication, whichever is later.
- The applicant must comply with all objections within 12 months.

## Pre-Grant Opposition
- Third parties may file a pre-grant opposition after publication but before the grant of the patent.

## Grant of Patent
- If all objections are cleared, the patent is granted.

## Post-Grant Opposition
- A post-grant opposition can be filed by any interested party within 12 months of the grant.
- The Controller reviews the opposition and makes a decision.
- If the opposition is rejected, the patent remains valid.
- If the opposition is accepted, the patent may be revoked or amended.

## Appeals and Revocation
- If dissatisfied with the Controller’s decision, the party can appeal to the *Appellate Board*.
- Further appeals can be made to the High Court.
- If the High Court revokes the patent, it is no longer valid.

# Duties of Patentee

## Maintenance of the Patent
- The patentee must ensure the maintenance of the patent by paying the *renewal* fees.
- The renewal fees must be *paid every year* until the end of the patent term.

## Time Limit for Renewal
- The renewal fee must be paid before the end of the preceding year.
- A *grace period* of ==6 months== is available **for late payment**.

## Working of the Patent
- > `matlab invention ko use karo nahi to legal notice aa jayega`
- The patented invention must be worked in India on a commercial scale.
- If the invention is not worked, a compulsory license may be issued.

# Patent Infringement

- The patentee can demand the *infringer* stop and *pay damages*.
- Offering a *royalty* is an option to settle the infringement.
- The patentee may ignore or postpone action.
- A lawsuit can be filed in federal court for infringement.

# Litigation vs Licensing

- **Litigation** is preferred if the goal is to stop a competitor from selling an infringing product.
- **Licensing** is suitable when seeking royalties for the use of an invention.

## Alternatives to Litigation

- Litigation is expensive, time-consuming, and disruptive.
- **Alternative dispute resolutions (ADR)** like mediation and arbitration offer cost-effective solutions.
    - **Mediation**: A neutral third party helps both parties negotiate a mutually acceptable agreement. It is non-binding and *focuses on settlement*.
    - **Arbitration**: A neutral arbitrator listens to both sides and makes a decision, which can be binding or non-binding depending on prior agreement. It is faster and less formal than litigation.
- Think of **mediation** like a teacher helping two kids agree on how to share a toy. The teacher doesn’t force a decision—both kids must agree.
- **Arbitration** is like a referee in a game who listens to both sides and then makes a final call. Sometimes, the decision must be followed (binding), and sometimes, the players can choose to accept it or not (non-binding).

# TRIPS: Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights

- The TRIPS Agreement ensures that countries follow global rules for protecting intellectual property. *India joined* in ==1995== and updated its laws to meet these standards. Now, trademarks, copyrights, designs, geographical indications, and integrated circuits all have legal protection in India.
